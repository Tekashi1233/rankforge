How to use this UTM builder

Paste the page you want people to land on, then fill in the three required fields. Source is where the click comes from, such as google, facebook or newsletter. Medium is the type of traffic, such as cpc, social or email. Campaign is the name of the push behind the link, such as spring-sale or weekly-tips. The two optional fields cover the finer detail: term holds the paid search keyword, and content tells two links in the same email apart, such as top-button and footer-link. The tagged URL builds itself as you type, and the copy button puts it on your clipboard. A preset fills in source and medium for the channel you picked, so you only have to name the campaign.

What are UTM parameters?

UTM stands for Urchin Tracking Module, named after Urchin, the analytics company Google bought in 2005. A UTM parameter is a small label added to the end of a link, after a question mark, in the form utm_source=facebook. Analytics tools read those labels when a visitor arrives and record them alongside the visit, so you can sort your traffic by where it came from instead of guessing. There are five of them, and three are required for a tag to be useful.

ParameterAnswersExample
utm_sourceWhere did the click come from?google, facebook, newsletter
utm_mediumWhat kind of traffic is it?cpc, social, email
utm_campaignWhich push is behind this link?spring-sale, weekly-tips
utm_termWhich search keyword triggered the ad?running-shoes
utm_contentWhich exact link was clicked?top-button, banner-a

A full tagged link looks like this: https://example.com/blog/my-post?utm_source=newsletter&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=weekly-tips&utm_content=top-button. The page itself does not change. A visitor who clicks it lands on the same page as everyone else. Only the report changes, because now Analytics knows the click came from your newsletter, from the email channel, from the weekly-tips campaign, and from the button at the top.

Why it matters

Without tags, most of your traffic arrives with no story attached. Analytics files a large share of it under "direct", which is the bucket for visits it cannot explain. That bucket mixes together people who typed your address by hand, people who clicked a link in an app that strips referrer data, and people who clicked your untagged Facebook post. You cannot tell which of those three did the work, so you cannot tell whether the post was worth the hour you spent on it.

Tags split that bucket open. Tag the link in your newsletter, tag the link in your Facebook post, tag the link in your ad, and a week later you can compare the three side by side: how many visits each one sent, how long those visitors stayed, and how many of them did what you wanted. That is the difference between knowing your ad spend brought 200 visits and knowing it brought 200 visits that left after four seconds. The first number flatters you. The second one tells you to fix the landing page.

Naming rules that keep your reports clean

UTM values are case-sensitive in Analytics. Facebook and facebook show up as two different sources, and Spring-Sale and spring-sale show up as two different campaigns. Pick one style and stick to it, and the simplest style is lowercase with hyphens. That is why this tool lowercases your values by default. Four rules cover the rest.

Write your names down somewhere, even a plain text file. When a second person on your team starts tagging links, a shared list of names is the only thing that stops your reports splitting into three spellings of the same campaign. Short names help too. A campaign name only needs to be clear enough that you recognise it in a report six months later.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Tagging links on your own site. UTM tags on internal links start a new session in Analytics, so one visit gets counted as two. Tag only links that bring people to your site from outside.
  • Mixing up source and medium. Source is the place, such as facebook. Medium is the type, such as social. Swapping them makes your reports unreadable, because the same place shows up under two different types.
  • Using a different spelling each week. Facebook, facebook and fb are three sources in your report, and none of them shows the real total. Pick one spelling and reuse it.
  • Leaving spaces in a value. A space in a UTM value gets encoded as %20, which works but makes the link long and ugly. Use hyphens instead.
  • Tagging every link you share. Your homepage link in your email signature does not need a tag, and neither does a link in a chat with a friend. Tag the links you want to measure, and leave the rest alone.
  • Using utm_term for anything but paid search. Term is for the keyword that triggered a search ad. Filling it in for a social post adds noise to a field that means something specific.

Do UTM parameters affect SEO?

No. Search engines ignore UTM parameters when they rank pages, and a tagged link points at the same page as the untagged one. Two things are worth doing anyway. First, keep a self-referencing canonical tag on the page, so search engines know the clean address is the main one. Our Canonical URL Generator builds one in seconds. Second, do not let tagged links get indexed as separate pages. If a tagged URL shows up in search results, it is usually because someone linked to the tagged version, and a canonical tag fixes that.

Frequently asked questions

What is a UTM parameter?

A label added to the end of a link, such as utm_source=facebook, that tells your analytics tool where a click came from. The five parameters are source, medium, campaign, term and content. They change nothing on the page itself; they only change what your reports show.

Do UTM parameters affect SEO?

No. Search engines ignore them when ranking pages. A tagged link and the same link without tags point at the same page. Keep a self-referencing canonical tag on the page so search engines treat the clean address as the main one.

Why does Analytics show my traffic as direct?

Direct is the bucket for visits Analytics cannot explain. Links shared in apps, chats and emails often arrive without referrer data, so they land there. Tagging those links with UTM parameters gives Analytics the story it was missing, and the visits show up under the source and campaign you named.

Should I tag links on my own site?

No. UTM tags on internal links start a new session in Analytics, so one visit gets counted as two and your numbers inflate. Tag only links that bring people to your site from outside, such as social posts, ads, emails and links on other sites.

Are UTM values case-sensitive?

Yes. Facebook and facebook are two different sources in Google Analytics, and Spring-Sale and spring-sale are two different campaigns. Use lowercase with hyphens everywhere, and write your names down so everyone on your team spells them the same way.

Is it safe to paste my URL here?

Yes. The tool runs entirely in your browser. Nothing you type is sent to a server, stored, or logged.

What if my URL already has UTM parameters?

The builder removes the old utm_source, utm_medium, utm_campaign, utm_term and utm_content values and adds the new ones, so you never end up with a link that carries two sets of tags. Any other parameters in the URL are left alone.

What does utm_content do?

It tells two links in the same campaign apart. If your newsletter has a button at the top and a text link at the bottom, give them different utm_content values, such as top-button and footer-link, and you can see which one people actually clicked.
