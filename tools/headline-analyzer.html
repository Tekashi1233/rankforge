Headline Analyzer

Paste a headline to see its length, word balance and score.

What does a headline analyzer do?

A headline analyzer checks a headline before you publish it. It measures the character and word count, looks at the mix of common, uncommon, power and emotional words, and checks for a number, a question, all caps and extra punctuation. The result is a score out of 100, plus a short list of things to fix.

Why headlines matter for clicks and SEO

A headline is often the first thing a reader sees in search results or on social media. If it is unclear, too long or too vague, people skip past it, even when the page behind it is good. Google shows about 50-60 characters before it cuts a title off in search results. That is common guidance, not a guarantee, and the exact cut-off varies by device and by query. A headline that fits, says what the page is about and sounds specific gives a reader a clear reason to click.

How to use this Headline Analyzer

Paste or type a headline into the box above. The score updates as you type, so you can try a shorter version, add a number or swap a word and watch the score change. Read the score and the checks below it, then try one or two variants and keep the version that is clearest, not just the one with the highest number.

How the score works

The score is out of 100 and comes from five parts:

  • Length - 30 points. Headlines of 50-60 characters score best. Shorter or longer headlines score less.
  • Word count - 15 points. Headlines of 6-12 words score best.
  • Power and emotional words - 20 points. Headlines with at least one power word and at least one emotional word score best.
  • Number or question - 15 points. A headline with a number, a question word or a question mark scores best.
  • Clarity - 20 points. Points are taken off for all caps and for repeated exclamation marks.

This is RankForge's own checklist, not Google's algorithm. Google does not publish a headline score, and no tool can predict a ranking from a title alone. Use the score as a quick check for length and clarity, then judge the headline on whether it fits the page.

Worked examples: rewriting three weak headlines

Scores are useful, but the rewrite is where the learning happens. Here are three headlines before and after, with the reason each change helps:

  • Before: "Tips for SEO" (12 characters, 3 words)
    After: "7 Simple SEO Tips That Help New Pages Get Found" (48 characters, 10 words)
    Why it works: the first version could be about anything. The rewrite says how many tips there are, who they are for, and what they do. It also lands in the length range that fits search results.
  • Before: "Our New Product Is Here!!!"
    After: "A Lighter Running Shoe Built for Daily Miles" (46 characters, 8 words)
    Why it works: the first version is all excitement and no information. Nobody searches for "our new product". The rewrite names the product and its one clear benefit, and it drops the exclamation marks that cost clarity points.
  • Before: "How to Do Keyword Research for Free in 2026: A Complete Step-by-Step Guide for Beginners" (91 characters)
    After: "Free Keyword Research: A Beginner's Step-by-Step Guide" (56 characters, 7 words)
    Why it works: the original tries to say everything and gets cut off in search results. The rewrite keeps the three things a reader cares about, free, beginner, and step-by-step, and drops the rest. The year can live in the article itself.

Notice that none of the rewrites chase the highest possible score. Each one trades a point or two for clarity, because a headline that scores 95 and misleads the reader is worse than one that scores 78 and tells the truth.

Headlines for search vs headlines for social media

A headline does two different jobs depending on where it appears. In search results it competes with nine other blue links, and the reader already typed the topic, so the winning headline puts the topic and the promise up front and fits in about 50 to 60 characters. On social media the reader did not ask for the topic, so the headline has to earn the stop: a specific result, a clear question, or a surprising fact. The same page can serve both. The title tag is what search shows, while the Open Graph title is what social platforms show, so you can keep the search title factual and let the social title lean a little further into curiosity. The one rule that never changes is honesty. A headline that promises something the page does not deliver earns a click and loses the reader, and a reader who feels tricked rarely comes back.

Common headline mistakes

  • Too vague to mean anything. "Ways to Improve" or "Everything You Need to Know" could describe any page on the internet. Name the topic and the outcome.
  • Clickbait the page cannot back up. "You Won't Believe What Happened Next" works once, then readers learn to skip your links. The headline should match the strongest real claim in the article.
  • All caps or shouty punctuation. It reads as yelling, it costs clarity points here, and it makes the result look less trustworthy next to calmer links.
  • Keyword stuffing. Repeating the same phrase twice in one headline helps nobody and reads badly. Say the topic once, clearly.
  • The same headline on every page. Category pages, tag pages, and similar posts end up sharing one generic title. Each page deserves its own headline that says what makes it different.
  • Clever wordplay nobody searches for. A pun can work for a loyal audience, but a new reader searching for an answer will not type it. Put the plain topic first and save the wordplay for the first line of the article.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good headline length?

Aim for about 50 to 60 characters so the full title usually fits in search results. Shorter than 40 often wastes space, longer than 70 often gets cut off.

Does this tool store my headline?

No. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is uploaded.

What are power words?

Short words that add feeling or urgency, like free, proven, simple, quick, and new. They help when they fit the topic, and they hurt when they sound like hype.

Should every headline have a number?

No. Numbers can help when the post really is a list or a set of steps. Forcing a number into a topic that is not a list usually reads worse.

Will a high score guarantee clicks?

No. A high score means the headline is clear, the right length, and specific. The topic, the search result around it, and the page itself still decide most clicks.

Should the headline and the title tag be the same?

They are often the same or very close. The title tag is what search results show, and the headline (usually the H1) is what readers see on the page. They can differ slightly, for example a shorter title tag for search, but they should cover the same topic so readers do not feel misled when they click through.

How many headline variants should I write?

Three to five is a good range. Write them, run each through the analyzer, and keep the one that is clearest and most specific, not just the highest scorer. If two variants score nearly the same, pick the one that sounds more like something a real person would say.
