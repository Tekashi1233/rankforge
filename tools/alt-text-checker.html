Why it matters

Alt text has two jobs, and both of them are easy to forget. The first is for people. A blind reader using a screen reader hears your alt text instead of seeing the picture, so a blank alt or a filename like IMG_4821.jpg tells them nothing, while a short plain description tells them what the rest of your readers see. The second is for search. Search engines cannot see a picture either, so the alt text is one of the few signals they have about what the image shows, and it is what Google shows in image search results. Good alt text serves both at once: one plain sentence that says what the image shows and why it is there.

The 125-character guideline comes from how screen readers read. Most of them pause or split the text around 125 characters, so a longer alt still gets read, but it gets chopped into pieces and is harder to follow. Treat 125 as the line to stay under, not a hard limit. A chart or a diagram sometimes needs more, and that is fine. The fix for a long description is a longer caption or a paragraph next to the image, not a longer alt attribute.

What good alt text looks like

Good alt text describes the image in plain words, front-loads the point, and stops. It does not start with "image of" or "picture of", because the screen reader already says it is an image. It does not repeat the caption or the heading next to it. It says what matters about the image in the context of the page, not every detail in it.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Leaving alt text blank on a picture that carries meaning. A blank alt tells a screen reader to skip the image entirely, which is right for a decorative divider and wrong for a product photo.
  • Pasting the filename as the alt text. IMG_4821.jpg or golden-retriever-puppy-final-v2.jpg says nothing to a listener and nothing useful to a search engine.
  • Stuffing keywords. Repeating "cheap running shoes" three times in one alt does not help rankings, and it sounds awful when read aloud.
  • Starting with "image of" or "picture of". The screen reader already announces the image, so those words waste the first ten characters.
  • Writing the same alt text on every image. If five product photos all say "blue t-shirt", a listener cannot tell them apart. Name what makes each one different.
  • Using "click here" on a linked image. Say where the link goes instead, such as "See pricing plans" or "Download the guide".
  • Describing every detail. A photo of a street scene does not need the colour of every car. Pick the detail a reader needs and stop.

Frequently asked questions

What is alt text?

Alt text is the short description of an image that sits in the alt attribute of an img tag. Screen readers read it aloud to people who cannot see the image, browsers show it when the image fails to load, and search engines use it to understand what the image shows.

How long should alt text be?

Stay under 125 characters where you can. Most screen readers pause or split the text around that length, so longer alt text gets harder to follow. A chart or diagram sometimes needs more, and that is fine: put the extra detail in a caption or a paragraph next to the image instead.

Can alt text be empty?

Yes, but only for decorative images that add nothing a reader needs, such as dividers, spacers and background patterns. An empty alt tells a screen reader to skip the image. A picture that carries meaning should always have alt text.

Does alt text help SEO?

Yes, in a modest way. Search engines use alt text to understand what an image shows, and it is what they show for the image in image search. It is not a ranking trick: keyword-stuffed alt text does not help, and the main gain is that your images can be found and understood at all.

Should I start alt text with "image of"?

No. A screen reader already announces that it is reading an image, so "image of" wastes the first words. Start with the subject itself, such as "Golden retriever puppy sitting on a red rug".

What alt text should a linked image have?

Say where the link goes, not what the picture looks like. A logo that links home should say "RankForge home page", and a button image that links to pricing should say "See pricing plans". The reader needs to decide whether to follow the link.
