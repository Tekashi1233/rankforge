What is keyword density?

Keyword density is the percentage of times a specific word or phrase appears in a piece of text compared to the total number of words. For example, if the word “SEO” appears 10 times in a 500-word article, its density is 2%. The idea originated in early search engine optimization, when ranking algorithms relied heavily on exact keyword matches. Today the concept is less rigid, but checking density remains a useful way to spot overuse or underuse of important terms.

Why keyword density still matters for search optimization

Modern search engines and AI answer systems evaluate content for relevance, helpfulness, and natural language. They no longer reward high densities or punish moderate ones the way older algorithms did. However, density analysis still helps writers in three practical ways:

  • Avoid keyword stuffing — When one term dominates a page, the text often feels forced and less readable. Search engines can interpret this as low-quality or manipulative content.
  • Confirm topical focus — Your primary topic words should appear naturally throughout the page. If they are missing or extremely rare, the page may not clearly signal its subject.
  • Improve clarity for readers and AI — Balanced language helps both human visitors and generative engines understand the main ideas quickly.

There is no universal “perfect” density. Most well-written pages land between 0.5% and 2.5% for primary keywords, but the real goal is natural, useful writing rather than hitting a number.

How to use this Keyword Density Checker

1. Paste the full body of your article, landing page, or product description into the box above.
2. The tool immediately counts meaningful words (short filler words such as “the”, “and”, “of” are ignored so results focus on content terms).
3. Review the table: each row shows a keyword, its raw count, and its density percentage relative to the total word count.
4. Look at the top terms. Do they match the topic you intended? Is any single word appearing so often that the text feels repetitive?

How to interpret the results manually

Start with the highest-ranked words. These should generally relate to your main topic. If an unrelated word appears high on the list, you may have drifted off-topic or repeated a secondary phrase too often. Next, check whether your primary keyword or phrase appears at all. If it is missing, consider adding it in natural places such as the introduction, a subheading, and the conclusion.

Watch for density above roughly 3–4%. While not an automatic penalty, high values often correlate with awkward phrasing. The best fix is almost always to rewrite sentences for better flow rather than simply deleting instances of the keyword. Finally, compare the results against a few top-ranking pages on the same topic. If their language feels more varied and conversational, aim for a similar balance.

Best practices for natural keyword use

  • Write the first draft without thinking about density. Focus on answering the reader’s question completely.
  • After drafting, run the text through this tool. Adjust only where the results reveal clear overuse or underuse.
  • Use synonyms and related phrases. Search engines understand semantic relationships, so variety improves both readability and relevance signals.
  • Place important terms in headings, the first 100 words, and image alt text when relevant — but only when it feels natural.
  • Prioritize user experience. A slightly lower density that produces clearer, more engaging content almost always outperforms forced repetition.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good keyword density?

There is no magic percentage. Use your main keyword naturally and focus on writing helpful content instead. Most high-quality pages fall between 0.5% and 2.5% for primary terms.

Are common words counted?

Short filler words like "the" and "and" are ignored so the results show meaningful terms.

Is my text saved?

No. The analysis runs entirely in your browser. Nothing is uploaded or stored on a server.

Does keyword density still affect rankings in 2026?

Indirectly. Extreme stuffing can harm quality signals, while completely missing core terms can weaken relevance. Natural language that clearly covers the topic is what search engines and AI systems reward.
