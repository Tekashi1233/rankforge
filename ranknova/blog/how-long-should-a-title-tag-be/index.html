How Long Should a Title Tag Be? A Practical Guide

A clear way to write concise page titles that stay readable in search results and useful to real visitors.

By RankNova · 7 minute read

A title tag gives a page its name in browser tabs and offers search engines a concise clue about the page. It is also a common source for the clickable headline shown in search results, although a search engine may choose different wording. A useful title works both as a relevance hint and as a small promise to someone deciding what to open. Name the subject plainly, sound natural, and set an honest expectation for the page.

There is no single character limit that guarantees a title will display in full. Search result layouts vary by device, query, language and the width of individual letters. A run of wide capitals occupies more room than the same number of narrow characters. Many editors aim for roughly 50 to 60 characters as a convenient drafting range, but it is a guide rather than a pass-or-fail rule. Put the main idea early and make sure the sentence remains meaningful if the end is shortened.

Begin with the page’s one main topic and the phrase a reader would use to describe it. Put that idea near the beginning without forcing an awkward exact-match phrase. Add a useful detail when it makes the page distinct, such as a maintained year, a location or a defined outcome. A brand can appear at the end when recognition matters, but should not crowd out the subject. Give each important page its own title rather than reusing a site-wide formula.

A counter cannot tell whether wording is truthful or readable. Repeating a keyword, stacking separators and adding exaggerated claims may make a title less trustworthy even if it fits a target. “Simple Rain Garden Guide | Northside Nursery” tells a reader what to expect; a string of near-identical phrases does not. If a page covers several related questions, name the central one in the title and answer the others in headings and body copy.

Use a three-part editing pass. Check that the title accurately describes the page, read it aloud to catch unnatural phrasing, and compare it with titles on related pages of your own site. Duplicate titles make pages harder to distinguish, particularly when their subjects overlap. A page-specific noun, audience or benefit often clears up the difference. Also check that a page title is not merely the site name repeated with a different suffix.

After a change, keep a note of the previous title and why you edited it. Search displays can change independently, so a replacement may not appear immediately and does not guarantee a ranking movement. Review impressions and click trends over a sensible period, considering seasonality, query mix and changes to the results page. Avoid editing many page elements at once if you want to learn which change may have mattered.

The durable goal is to make the subject unmistakable, keep wording specific and use only as many words as the page’s promise needs.

A useful next step

A final practical check is to view the page as part of a group. If two titles begin with the same phrase, decide whether they answer separate questions or should be combined. For a service page, location and offering may be the clearest distinction; for an article series, each installment needs its own subject rather than only a sequence number. Keep the wording aligned with the visible heading, while recognizing that a browser title and an on-page H1 can serve related but not identical jobs. Review a sample of important pages whenever you change a title template, because a global template can create repeated text across many URLs.
