What Is Heading Structure and How to Check It

A clear H1–H6 hierarchy helps readers scan the page, helps search engines understand topics, and helps AI systems extract the right passages.

What heading structure means

Heading structure is the ordered outline created by the H1, H2, H3 (and lower) tags on a page. The H1 is the main title of the page. H2s mark major sections. H3s mark subsections under an H2, and so on. When the levels are sequential and logical, the page has a predictable outline.

Why it matters for SEO and GEO

Search engines use headings as strong signals of topic and subtopic. AI answer engines often prefer content that is already organised into clear sections with descriptive headings, because those sections are easier to quote or summarise. Accessibility tools also rely on the heading tree so users can jump between sections.

Rules that still hold in 2026

  • Use exactly one H1 per page (or per main content region).
  • Do not skip levels (avoid jumping from H1 straight to H3).
  • Write descriptive headings that state the topic of the section, not vague labels like “More info”.
  • Keep the visual style consistent with the semantic level (do not style a paragraph to look like an H2 without using the real tag).

How to check your headings

You can view source and search for <h1> through <h6>, use browser developer tools, or paste the page HTML into a free checker. RankForge includes a Heading Structure Checker that lists the outline, counts H1s, and flags skipped levels in one click.

Quick fix checklist

  1. Ensure there is one clear H1 that matches the main topic (and ideally the title tag).
  2. Turn major section titles into H2s.
  3. Nest genuine subsections under the correct parent level.
  4. Remove or demote extra H1s that were only used for styling.
  5. Re-check the outline after editing.

A quick example: a clear outline vs a broken one

Take a guide about heading structure. A clear outline reads: H1 “What Is Heading Structure and How to Check It”, then H2 “What heading structure means”, H2 “Why it matters for SEO and GEO”, and H2 “How to check your headings”. Each H2 covers one major idea, and any detail underneath it becomes an H3.

A broken version of the same page might jump from the H1 straight to an H3, then use a second H1 for the checklist at the end. The content is the same, but the outline no longer shows which sections belong together. A reader scanning the page, a screen reader jumping between headings, and a search engine building a topic map all have to work harder to follow it.

When you check a page, read the outline on its own, without the body text. If the headings alone tell the story of the page in order, the structure is doing its job.

A clean heading structure is one of the fastest improvements you can make for both human readers and machines.
