How to Write Alt Text That Helps SEO and Accessibility

You publish a post about your bakery and add a photo of the team outside the shop, flour on their aprons, laughing. A reader who is blind visits the page, and her screen reader reads out the only thing you gave the photo: IMG_4821.jpg. Alt text is the fix, and it takes about thirty seconds per image.

The alt attribute is a short description of an image, tucked inside the img tag. A screen reader reads it aloud in place of the picture, a browser shows it when the image fails to load, and Google reads it to work out what the picture shows. One line of writing does all three jobs. Our Alt Text Checker tests the line you wrote against the 125-character guideline, or writes one for you from a description of the image.

Quick answer: Alt text is the short description of an image in the alt attribute of an img tag. Keep it under 125 characters, name the subject and the one detail that matters, and leave out "image of". Screen readers read it aloud, browsers show it when the image fails, and search engines use it to understand the picture.

What alt text actually does

Start with the person, not the search engine. A blind reader does not see your images at all. The screen reader she uses reads the page out loud, and when it reaches a picture it reads the alt text. A blank alt means the reader skips past it in silence, and a filename means she hears "IMG_4821.jpg" read out letter by letter. Neither tells her what the picture shows.

The same text shows up in two other places. When an image fails to load, slow connection or broken file, the browser shows the alt text where the picture should be, so the reader still gets the point. And search engines read it too. Google cannot see a photograph, so the alt text is one of the few signals it has about what an image shows, and it is the caption it shows for the image in image search results. Write one good line and all three readers get what they need. That is also the limit of what alt text can do. It will not rescue a weak page, and its job is to make the image understood, not to carry your keyword plan.

The 125-character guideline

You will see the number 125 everywhere alt text is discussed. It is not a rule from Google or from HTML, and nothing breaks if you go past it. It comes from screen readers. Most of them pause or split the text at around 125 characters, so a longer alt still gets read, but it arrives in pieces and is harder to follow.

In practice, most good alt text lands well under the line. A product photo needs a name and a colour. A team photo needs the people and the place. A chart needs the point it makes. If you find yourself past 125, ask whether the extra words belong in the alt at all. A chart that needs a paragraph of explanation is better served by a caption under the image, with a short alt.

There is one case where long is fine. A diagram or an infographic that holds real information sometimes needs more than a sentence. Write the short version in the alt and put the full version beside the image where everyone can read it.

How to write alt text that works

Write the way you would describe the image to a friend on the phone. Name the subject first, then add the one detail that matters. "Golden retriever puppy sitting on a red rug" works because the subject is in the first two words and the detail that makes the photo worth using is right behind it. Put the point first, because a listener cannot skim. If the picture is a chart, the point is the trend, not the chart type: "Bar chart showing sales rising from 40 to 120 orders a month in 2026" tells the reader what the chart means, while "chart" tells them nothing.

Write for the page the image sits on, not for the image in isolation. The same photo needs different alt text in different places. A photo of a baker sliding bread into an oven needs "Baker sliding sourdough into the oven" on a page about your bakery, and a different line on a page about your baking method. The context decides which detail matters, so read the paragraph around the image before you write the alt.

Keep it plain. Alt text is not the place for jokes, brand slogans or sales copy. A product photo for a blue cotton t-shirt needs "Blue cotton t-shirt with a round neck", not "Our amazing premium tee, the softest shirt you will ever own". Leave out "image of" and "picture of" too: the screen reader already says it is an image, so those words waste the first ten characters. The reader wants to know what the shirt looks like. Save the sales pitch for the text beside it, where everyone can read it.

Different images need different alt text

Not every image plays the same part on a page, and the alt text should match the part it plays.

Kind of imageWhat the alt text should doExample
Informative photoName the subject and the detail that mattersGolden retriever puppy sitting on a red rug
Decorative imageEmpty alt text, so screen readers skip italt=""
Linked imageSay where the link goesSee pricing plans
Product photoName the product and what sets it apartBlue cotton t-shirt with a round neck
Chart or graphState the point the chart makesBar chart showing sales rising from 40 to 120 orders a month in 2026
Complex diagramShort alt plus a long description beside the imageMap of delivery zones in the city, full list below

The decorative row catches people out. A divider, a spacer or a background pattern adds nothing a listener needs, and the right alt text is none at all. An empty alt attribute, alt="", tells the screen reader to skip the image without a pause. That is not laziness. It is the correct answer for a picture with no job to do. The mistake is leaving alt empty on a picture that does have a job, like a product photo or a chart.

Mistakes that make alt text useless

Stuffing keywords into the alt is the worst habit. "Cheap running shoes, best running shoes, running shoes sale, buy running shoes" reads like a spam list, and that is how a screen reader will read it too, word by word. Search engines stopped rewarding that trick years ago, and the only person it punishes now is the listener. One mention of the product is plenty.

Pasting the filename in as the alt text is the next habit to drop. IMG_4821.jpg and golden-retriever-puppy-final-v2.jpg are not descriptions, and a listener hears every character. If you catch yourself doing this, it usually means the image was never given a proper name.

Repeating the same alt text on every image on a page is the third. Five product photos that all say "blue t-shirt" are five photos a listener cannot tell apart. Name what makes each one different: the colour, the fit, the detail in the shot.

Writing "click here" on a linked image is the fourth. A listener deciding whether to follow a link needs to know where it goes, not that it is clickable. "See pricing plans" or "Download the guide" does the job.

Leaving alt blank on a picture that matters is the last one. A product photo with no alt text is invisible to a listener and to image search at the same time. If the image carries meaning, give it words. If it does not, give it an empty alt on purpose, so the screen reader knows you meant to skip it.

How to check your alt text

Read your own alt text out loud. If it sounds like a sentence you would say to a friend, it is probably fine. If it sounds like a list of keywords or a filename, it is not. Frequently asked questions

What is alt text?

The short description of an image that lives in the alt attribute of an img tag. Screen readers read it aloud, browsers show it when the image fails to load, and search engines use it to understand what the image shows.

How long should alt text be?

Under 125 characters where you can. Screen readers tend to pause or split the text around that length. A chart or diagram sometimes needs more: put the extra detail in a caption or a paragraph next to the image.

Does alt text help SEO?

Yes, in a modest way. It helps search engines understand the image and show it in image search. Keyword stuffing does not help, and alt text will not rescue a weak page on its own.

Can alt text be empty?

Yes, for decorative images only. An empty alt tells a screen reader to skip the image. A picture that carries meaning should always have alt text.

Should alt text repeat the caption?

No. If the caption already says what the image shows, the alt text should add nothing, or the image is decorative and the alt should be empty. Repeating the same words twice wastes a listener's time.

Do I need alt text on every image?

Every image that carries meaning, yes: logos, product photos, charts and photos in articles. Decorative images need an empty alt attribute instead, so the screen reader skips them.
