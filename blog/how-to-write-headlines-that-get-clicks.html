How to Write Headlines That Get Clicks (Without Sounding Like Clickbait)

Quick answer: A good headline is specific, about 50 to 60 characters long, says what the reader gets, and matches the page it leads to. Test it with the Headline Analyzer before you publish, and rewrite it if it sounds vague or promises more than the page delivers.

You spent a week on a post. You researched it, wrote it, rewrote the weak parts, and finally hit publish. Then you shared it. And almost no one clicked.

That stings. But most of the time the post was not the problem. The headline was. People never got far enough to find out how good the writing was, because the line they saw first gave them no reason to open it. I have done this myself, more than once, and the fix was rarely a better post. It was a clearer headline. Run your next one through our Headline Analyzer before you publish and you will catch the weak ones early.

Why most headlines get ignored

Most headlines get ignored because they could belong to anyone. "Tips for Better Writing" tells the reader nothing. A person scrolling past ten results has no reason to pick the one that says the least.

Vague headlines also hide the post behind them. If you wrote a careful guide to writing shorter sentences, a headline like "Writing Advice" buries that. The reader cannot tell if your post answers their question, so they pick a result that spells it out. Scanning a results page is fast, and people trust the headline that sounds like it knows what they need.

There is a second problem. A lot of headlines repeat the same words as every other result. "Ultimate Guide to SEO", "The Best SEO Tips", "SEO Made Easy". When five results sound the same, the click goes to the site the reader already knows, or to the one result that says something different. Be more exact about what your page actually covers.

What a good headline actually does

A good headline does three things, and it does them in one short line. It says what the page is about. It says what the reader gets from opening it. And it tells the truth about what is inside.

A headline that promises a full guide and leads to three short paragraphs will get the click once, and then the reader leaves and does not come back. Trust builds slowly on a small site.

Look at a headline you like and ask what it promises. "How to Write Headlines That Get Clicks" promises a method, not a secret. "How Long Should a Meta Description Be?" promises one clear answer. Both work because the reader knows what they will find before they click. If your headline needs the post to explain it, the headline is not finished yet.

How long should a headline be?

Google shows about 50 to 60 characters of a title before it cuts it off. That is common guidance, not a hard rule. Google sometimes shows more, sometimes less, and it can rewrite a title entirely. Still, 50 to 60 characters is a useful target when you are writing one.

Longer than 70 characters often gets cut off in the middle of the point. Take this headline: "10 Ways to Write Better Headlines for Your Blog Posts in 2026". I counted it by hand. It is 61 characters, which already sits past the comfortable range, and the last words add nothing. Cut it down to "10 Ways to Write Better Headlines". That version is 33 characters. Same promise, half the length, and nothing important is lost.

Counting characters takes ten seconds, and it is worth doing every time. Paste the headline into our Word Counter and check the count before you publish. If the cut lands in the middle of a useful word, trim from the end first. The first words carry the most weight, because they are the ones a reader sees even when the rest is hidden.

Numbers, questions, and specifics that help

Numbers help when the post really is a list. "7 Headline Mistakes That Cost You Clicks" works because the reader knows what to expect: seven mistakes, each one named. That headline is 40 characters, and the number does real work in it. It sets the shape of the post before anyone opens it.

But a number does not fix a vague headline. "10 Tips for Success" is still vague with a number on the front. And forcing a number into a post that is not a list usually reads worse. Use a number when the count is true and the reader gains from knowing it up front. Skip it when it is not.

Questions work the same way. A question headline works when the reader really has that question. "How Long Should a Meta Description Be?" works because plenty of people type exactly that into Google. A question nobody asks does not pull anyone in.

Specifics are the part most headlines are missing. A number, a time, a tool name, a named audience. "Headline Tips for New Bloggers" says more than "Headline Tips". "Write a Headline in 10 Minutes" says more again. One real detail beats a pile of vague praise words.

Power words and emotional words

Power words are the small words headline guides love to list: free, proven, simple, quick. They can help, in small doses, when they fit the topic. "A Simple Way to Test Headlines" sounds honest if the method really is simple. "A Proven Way to Double Your Traffic" does not, because no headline method can promise that.

This whole area is overrated. A strong word cannot rescue a vague headline, and a plain headline that says exactly what the post covers will often beat a hyped one. Readers have seen "amazing" and "incredible" so many times that the words barely register now.

Use a power word only if you would use it in normal speech about your post. If you would not say "this is a proven method" to a friend, do not put it in the headline. And if the page cannot back the word up, leave it out. Hype backfires in a quiet way. The reader clicks, finds less than promised, and files your site under "not worth it".

How to test a headline before you publish

Testing a headline takes a few minutes, and it is the step most people skip. Do not trust your gut alone. You wrote the post, so the headline makes sense to you. It has to make sense to someone who has never seen it. A quick run through our Readability Score Checker helps too, since hard-to-read copy often sits under a hard-to-read headline.

Start by writing three variants of the same headline. Not three different topics, three ways of saying the same thing. One can be plain, one can carry a number, one can carry a question. Paste each one into the Headline Analyzer and read the score and the notes it gives you. The score is a guide, not a verdict. Look at what it flags, then fix the flagged part and test again.

Then read the best version out loud. If you stumble over it, or if it sounds like a slogan, rewrite it in plainer words. A headline should sound like something you would say to a reader, not something printed on a poster. Last, open the post and check that the headline matches what is actually on the page. If the headline promises a list and the post has no list, change one of them.

Common headline mistakes to avoid

Being vague. "Some Thoughts on Writing" could be about anything. Name the topic and the payoff, or the reader moves on.

Writing in all caps. All caps reads like shouting, and it is harder to scan. Use normal capitalisation and let the words do the work.

Being too clever. A pun that needs the post to explain it will lose the reader before they open the post. Clever is fine when the meaning is still clear at first glance. If it is not, trade the joke for a plain line.

Promising what the page does not deliver. This is the one that costs you repeat readers. A headline that promises a full guide needs a full guide behind it. If the post is short, write a headline that fits a short post.

Stuffing keywords. "Headline Writing Tips, Headline Examples, Best Headlines" reads like a tag list, not a headline. One clear topic phrase is enough. Your Meta Tag Generator can show you how the title and description look together, and if the title reads like a keyword list, that is the sign to rewrite it.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a headline be?

About 50 to 60 characters works well for search results. Shorter than 40 often wastes space, and longer than 70 often gets cut off.

Should I use a number in every headline?

No. Use a number when the post really is a list or a set of steps. Forcing one into other topics usually reads worse.

Do power words really work?

They can help when they fit the topic and sound honest. They hurt when the headline promises more than the page delivers.

Is title case or sentence case better?

Either can work. Pick one style, use it the same way across your site, and check our guide on title case vs sentence case for the rules.

Will a high headline score guarantee clicks?

No. A clear, specific headline helps, but the topic, the other results on the page, and the page itself still decide most clicks.

Try it on your next headline

Pick the next post you plan to publish and write three headlines for it before you write anything else. Keep each one under 60 characters, give it one real detail, and read it out loud. Then paste the best one into the Headline Analyzer and see what it flags. For the rest of the page, check how long a meta description should be so the title and description work together.

If the score is low, do not throw the headline away. Change one thing at a time: trim the end, swap in a number that is true, or name the reader the post is for. Test again. Small edits like that usually lift a weak headline more than starting over does.
