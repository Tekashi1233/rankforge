How Many Words Should a Blog Post Be? Word Count and SEO

Quick answer: There is no ideal word count. Google has said length is not a ranking factor, so write as many words as it takes to answer the reader's question completely.

Few SEO questions cause as much worry as word count. Should every post be 2,000 words? Is 500 too thin? The honest answer is that there is no magic number. Length matters only as far as it helps you answer the reader's question completely. This guide explains how to choose a sensible length for each post.

What Google says

Google has said that word count is not a ranking factor and that there is no minimum or maximum length. Pages rank because they satisfy the search, not because they hit a number. A short page that fully answers a quick question can outrank a long one that wanders.

Why long posts often do well anyway

Studies of ranking pages often find that top results tend to be fairly long. That is mostly a side effect: complex topics need more room, and thorough pages naturally attract more links, shares and visits. Length is a result of depth, not a cause of rankings, so padding a post will not help.

Why there is no magic number

The 2,000-word rule shows up in a lot of SEO advice, but nobody at Google has ever given a target number. The idea spread because studies kept finding that pages on page one were often long — but those studies measured correlation, not cause. A page about a complex topic, like choosing a mortgage, needs room to explain terms, compare options and answer follow-up questions. A page answering "what is a slug" does not. If you force the short answer to 2,000 words, you end up repeating yourself or adding sections nobody asked for, and readers notice. Google's own advice has stayed the same for years: write for the reader, cover the topic, and stop when it is covered. A useful test is to read your draft and ask whether every section answers a question a real reader would ask. If a section exists only to add words, cut it.

Typical lengths by content type

  • Quick answers and news: 300 to 700 words.
  • Standard blog posts and how-tos: 800 to 1,500 words.
  • In-depth guides and comparisons: 1,500 to 3,000 words or more.
  • Product or tool pages: as long as needed to explain it clearly, often 300 to 800 words.

These are guidelines, not rules. Use them as a starting point and adjust to the topic.

How to choose the right length

  1. Search your target keyword and open the top results.
  2. Note how they are structured and what questions they answer.
  3. List anything important they miss.
  4. Write enough to cover all of it clearly, and stop there.

If your outline covers the topic better than competitors, length will take care of itself.

A worked example: planning length from the search results

Suppose you want to write about “how to check keyword density”. Search the phrase and open the top three results. One is a 400-word definition, one is a 900-word how-to with screenshots, and one is a 2,000-word guide that also covers tools and common mistakes. The question behind the search is practical — people want to check a page and fix it — so the 400-word definition leaves them short, while the 2,000-word guide may be more than a beginner needs.

A sensible target is around 1,000 to 1,200 words: a short definition, a step-by-step check, one worked example, and a short section on what to do if the density is too high or too low. That covers the question fully without repeating the longest guide. Before you publish, paste the draft into the Word Counter to confirm the length and reading time, then read it once through and cut anything that does not help the reader finish the task.

How to judge a competitor's length before you write

Before you write a word, look at what already ranks. Search your keyword and open the top three or four results. For each one, note two things: how long it is and what it actually covers. A quick way to measure length is to copy the main text into the Word Counter and check the number. Then write down the questions each page answers and, more importantly, the ones it skips. If the top result is 1,800 words but spends half of it on background a beginner does not need, you can beat it with 1,000 focused words. If all three results are short and leave the same question unanswered — say, what to do when the answer does not work — that gap is your opening, and your post will naturally run longer because it covers more. Length decided this way is not a guess; it comes from the gap between what readers need and what already exists.

Common mistakes when choosing length

Length goes wrong in predictable ways. Avoid these:

  • Copying the longest competitor word for word. Matching their length without matching their usefulness just produces a longer weak post.
  • Adding a long introduction. If the first 200 words do not help the reader, most will leave before the useful part starts. State the answer, then explain.
  • Treating every post the same. A news update and a buying guide are different jobs; giving both 1,500 words wastes effort on one and starves the other.
  • Counting words instead of questions answered. A 900-word post that answers five real questions beats a 2,000-word post that answers one twice.

Quality over padding

Do not repeat points, add long introductions or fill space with vague sentences just to reach a target. Readers leave quickly when they feel their time is wasted. Use short paragraphs, clear subheadings, lists and examples so even a long post is easy to scan.

When a short post wins

Some posts should stay short, and making them longer hurts. A news update, such as a tool adding a new feature, only needs the facts and a screenshot — 300 words is plenty. A definition page that answers one question in two paragraphs can rank well and is exactly what a reader in a hurry wants. Tool and product pages are the same: a password generator page needs a short explanation, a few tips and a clear button, not a 1,500-word essay. The sign that a post should stay short is when you cannot name a second real question the reader still has after the first answer. If the reader's job is done in 400 words, publish 400 words. A short post that does its job beats a long one that makes the reader hunt for the answer.

Frequently asked questions

Is word count a Google ranking factor?

No. Google has said that word count is not a ranking factor and that there is no minimum or maximum length.

How long should a standard blog post be?

Standard blog posts and how-tos are typically 800 to 1,500 words, while in-depth guides and comparisons often run 1,500 to 3,000 words or more. These are guidelines, not rules.

How do I choose the right length for a post?

Search your target keyword, open the top results, note the questions they answer, list what they miss, and write enough to cover it all clearly.

How long does it take to read a 1,500-word post?

Most adults read around 200 to 250 words per minute, so a 1,500-word post takes about six to seven minutes.

Is a 300-word blog post too short?

Not if it answers the question fully. A short news update, definition or announcement can rank well. What matters is whether the reader leaves satisfied, not the number at the bottom of the page.

Should I update old posts to make them longer?

Only if the topic has grown or readers are asking questions the post does not answer. Adding words for the sake of length does not help; adding a missing section or a worked example does.

Conclusion

Write as many words as the topic deserves and no more. Focus on answering the question better than anyone else, and keep your structure easy to read. For ideas on topics, see How to Do Keyword Research for Free.
