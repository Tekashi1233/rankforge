Title Case vs Sentence Case: Which Is Better for Headlines?

Quick answer: Neither style is better for SEO, because search engines do not treat capitalization as a ranking signal. Pick one style and use it consistently across your titles and headings.

Headlines can be written in different capitalization styles, and the two most common are title case and sentence case. Neither is wrong, and neither is a ranking factor, but consistency makes your site look professional. This guide explains both styles and how to choose.

What is title case?

In title case, the main words in a headline start with a capital letter. Example: "How to Write a Strong Meta Description". Short, minor words such as articles, short prepositions and conjunctions usually stay lowercase unless they are the first or last word.

What is sentence case?

In sentence case, only the first word and proper nouns are capitalized, exactly as in a normal sentence. Example: "How to write a strong meta description". Words like names, places and brands, such as Google or London, keep their capitals.

Main rules for title case

  • Capitalize the first and last words.
  • Capitalize nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs and pronouns.
  • Keep articles (a, an, the), coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or) and short prepositions (in, on, at, of) lowercase.
  • Style guides such as AP, Chicago and APA differ on details, especially on which prepositions count as short, so choose one and stay with it.

Title case is not one rule: AP, Chicago and MLA

There is no single title case. The big style guides agree on the main words but split on the small ones, and the split is almost always about prepositions. The three guides you will meet most often are AP, used in news, Chicago, used in books, and MLA, used in humanities essays, and they draw the line in different places.

Take one headline and run it through all three: "how to turn a small blog into a full time business".

AP: "How to Turn a Small Blog Into a Full-Time Business". AP capitalises every word of four letters or more, so "into" takes a capital.
Chicago: "How to Turn a Small Blog into a Full-Time Business". Chicago capitalises prepositions of five letters or more, so the four-letter "into" stays lowercase.
MLA: "How to Turn a Small Blog into a Full-Time Business". MLA keeps every preposition lowercase, whatever its length, so it matches Chicago on this headline.

The guides split again on longer prepositions. Take "a guide to writing about seo for beginners": AP and Chicago both write "A Guide to Writing About SEO for Beginners", because "about" is five letters long, while MLA writes "A Guide to Writing about SEO for Beginners", with "about" in lowercase. None of the three capitalises the "to" in "how to", because "to" in an infinitive stays lowercase in every guide.

For a website, none of these guides is the law. Pick the one your readers expect — AP if your site reads like news, Chicago if it reads like a book, MLA if it reads like an essay — and apply it the same way on every page. A converter will not know which guide you follow, so check its output against your chosen guide before you publish.

The tricky words: articles, short prepositions and hyphenated words

The words that cause the most mistakes in title case are the small ones, and each kind has its own rule.

Articles. "A", "an" and "the" stay lowercase in the middle of a title, in every guide. The only exception is when one of them is the first or last word, as in "The Art of War".

Short prepositions. "In", "on", "at", "of", "to", "by" and "for" stay lowercase too — unless they are the first or last word. That is why it is "What to Look For" and not "What to Look for": the last word always takes a capital.

The "to" in an infinitive. In "How to Cook Rice", "to" stays lowercase in every guide, because it belongs to the verb, not to the list of words being capitalised. The same goes for "How to Start a Blog".

Hyphenated words. Capitalise both parts when each part is a word in its own right: "Full-Time", "Follow-Up", "Well-Known". When the second part is a small word, it stays lowercase, as in "Run-of-the-Mill", where "of" and "the" stay down and only "Run" and "Mill" take capitals.

Phrasal verbs. In "How to Log In to Your Account", "In" takes a capital even though "in" is normally lowercase, because here it is part of the verb "log in", not a preposition. If you are unsure whether a word is a preposition or part of the verb, try reading the headline without it: "log to your account" makes no sense, so "in" belongs to the verb and takes a capital.

Which is better for SEO?

Neither. Search engines do not treat capitalization as a ranking signal. What matters is clarity and click appeal. Some people find title case looks more formal and eye-catching in search results, while others find sentence case easier to read and friendlier. Test what suits your audience. How to choose

  • Title case suits traditional publications, formal brands and book-style titles.
  • Sentence case suits modern, conversational sites, apps and documentation, and is simpler to apply correctly.
  • Consistency matters more than the choice. Use the same style for all titles and headings across your site.

Sentence case: "What is keyword density and why does it matter?"

Where the choice shows up: search results, social posts and email

The case you choose shows up in more places than your page headings, and each place has its own habits.

In search results, title case can make a listing look more formal and easier to scan, which suits guides and reference pages. Sentence case can feel friendlier, which suits blogs and personal sites. Google sometimes rewrites the title it shows, so the style you pick is a nudge to readers, not a guarantee of how it appears.

On social media, sentence case usually fits better, because posts read like something a person wrote. A title-case headline shared on X or Facebook can look like a press release. On LinkedIn the reverse is often true: title case matches the more formal tone of the feed.

Email subject lines follow the same split. Newsletters usually use sentence case, because it reads like a message from a person, while formal announcements often use title case. Whatever you pick, use the same style for the subject line and the headline of the page it links to, so the reader is not surprised when they click through.

Common title-case mistakes

Most title-case errors come from capitalizing too much, not too little. When every word starts with a capital, the headline looks shouty and the important words stop standing out. The usual culprits are short words that should stay lowercase: "a", "an", "the", "and", "but", "or", "in", "on", "at" and "of". Compare "How To Write A Strong Meta Description" with the correct "How to Write a Strong Meta Description" — the second version is easier to scan because only the main words draw the eye.

Two other mistakes are easy to miss. First, forgetting to capitalize the last word: in title case the last word always takes a capital, even if it is a short preposition, as in "What to Look For". Second, lowercasing a proper noun or an acronym by accident after running text through a converter. "SEO", "Google" and "London" keep their capitals in both styles, so always read a converted headline once before you publish it.

A quick checklist before you publish a headline

Use this short check every time you write a title or heading, and most capitalization problems disappear:

  • Pick one style for the whole site. Decide title case or sentence case once, write it down in your style notes, and do not switch between pages.
  • Capitalize the first and last words. This rule holds in title case no matter how short those words are.
  • Check proper nouns and acronyms. Names, places, brands and abbreviations such as SEO, HTML and PDF keep their capitals in either style.
  • Read the headline out loud. If a capitalized word looks wrong or a lowercase word looks like a typo, it probably is.
  • Match the heading to the page. The headline, the page title and the slug should all describe the same topic, so a reader who clicks through is not surprised.
