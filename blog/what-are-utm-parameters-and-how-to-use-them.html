What Are UTM Parameters and How to Use Them (Without Messing Up Your Analytics)

You post the same link in two places. One goes on your Facebook page in the morning, the other goes out in your newsletter that evening. By Friday your site has 300 extra visits and a few sales, and you open Analytics to find out which post did the work. The report says most of it came from direct traffic. Direct. As if 300 people typed your address into the browser by hand.

They did not. Some of them clicked the Facebook post, some clicked the newsletter, and a few probably clicked a link a friend forwarded in a chat app. Your reports cannot tell them apart, because nothing in the links says where each click came from. UTM parameters are that missing mark. Our UTM Campaign Builder adds them to a link in seconds, and the rest of this guide explains what they do and how to use them without wrecking your data.

Quick answer: UTM parameters are five short labels you add to the end of a link: utm_source, utm_medium, utm_campaign, utm_term and utm_content. They tell your analytics tool where a click came from and which push sent it. The page stays the same; only your reports change. Use lowercase names with hyphens, and tag only links from outside your site.

What UTM parameters actually are

UTM stands for Urchin Tracking Module, named after Urchin, the analytics company Google bought in 2005 and built Google Analytics on. A UTM parameter is a label stuck on the end of a link, after a question mark, written as utm_source=facebook. Your analytics tool reads the labels when a visitor arrives and files the visit under them. Nothing about the page changes: the same page loads for a tagged visitor and an untagged one. The only difference is what your report says afterwards.

ParameterAnswersExample
utm_sourceWhere did the click come from?google, facebook, newsletter
utm_mediumWhat kind of traffic is it?cpc, social, email
utm_campaignWhich push is behind this link?spring-sale, weekly-tips
utm_termWhich search keyword triggered the ad?running-shoes
utm_contentWhich exact link was clicked?top-button, banner-a

Here is what one looks like in full: https://example.com/blog/my-post?utm_source=newsletter&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=weekly-tips&utm_content=top-button. Read it from left to right and it tells a small story: the click came from the newsletter, arrived by email, belonged to the weekly-tips campaign, and came from the button at the top of the message. Source, medium and campaign are the three you will use every time. Term and content cover finer detail.

Why your reports lie without them

Most analytics tools sort traffic by source, and they can only sort what they can see. A click from a Google search carries a referrer, so it lands in the right bucket. A click from inside an app or an email often carries nothing, so it lands in direct. Direct is the drawer for visits the tool cannot explain, and it mixes together people who typed your address by hand and people who clicked a link in WhatsApp or Instagram.

That mix is the problem. If direct holds 40 percent of your traffic, you cannot say whether your Instagram posts bring visitors or your newsletter does. You might keep paying for ads that send nobody, or stop writing a newsletter that quietly sends half your sales. Tag the newsletter link and the Instagram link differently, wait a week, and the report shows each one on its own line.

The five parameters, one by one

utm_source names the place: the site or sender, such as google, facebook, instagram or newsletter. Keep it to one word where you can, and spell it the same way every time. Google and google are two different sources in your reports, so pick lowercase and stay with it.

utm_medium names the type of traffic: cpc for paid search, social for social posts, email for newsletters, referral for links on other sites. Medium is the field people mix up most, because it feels like it should hold the place. It does not. The place goes in source, and the type goes in medium.

utm_campaign names the push behind the link: the sale, the launch, or the series, such as spring-sale, weekly-tips or product-launch. This is the name you will read most often in reports, so make it clear enough that you still know what it meant six months from now.

utm_term holds the search keyword that triggered a paid ad, such as running-shoes. It only belongs on paid search links. Filling it in for a social post adds noise to a field with a narrow job.

utm_content tells two links in the same campaign apart. Your newsletter might have a button at the top and a text link at the bottom: give them different content values, such as top-button and footer-link, and you learn which one people click. If a link is the only one in its campaign, leave content out.

How to build a tagged link

Start with the page you want people to land on. Paste it into the builder, then fill in source, medium and campaign. Add term if the link is a paid search ad, and content if you need to tell two links in the same message apart. The builder puts the labels after a question mark, joins them with ampersands, and encodes any spaces as %20.

Say you run a small shop and you send a newsletter about a sale on running shoes. The page is https://example.com/sale/shoes, the source is newsletter, the medium is email, the campaign is spring-sale, and the content is top-button, because it sits in the button at the top of the message. The tagged link is https://example.com/sale/shoes?utm_source=newsletter&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=spring-sale&utm_content=top-button. Paste it into the button and send. When someone clicks, Analytics files the visit under newsletter, email, spring-sale and top-button, and you can compare it with the footer link next week.

If your page address already holds a question mark, the builder adds the tags with an ampersand instead, so nothing breaks. If the address already carries old UTM tags, the builder swaps them for the new ones rather than stacking a second set on top. Naming rules that keep your reports clean

The labels only help if they stay consistent. A report with facebook, Facebook and fb as three separate sources tells you nothing, because the real total is split three ways. Four rules stop that, and they take less time to follow than to fix later.

First, use lowercase everywhere. Analytics treats capitals as different values, so Facebook and facebook never merge on their own. Second, use hyphens instead of spaces. A space gets encoded as %20, which works but makes the link long and ugly. Third, reuse the same names. If your source is newsletter this week, it should still be newsletter next month, not mail or email-blast. Fourth, keep names short. A campaign called weekly-tips is easy to read in a report. A campaign called weekly-tips-issue-42-november-2026-final-v2 is not.

Write the names down in a notes app, so the next person who tags a link spells them the same way.

Mistakes that wreck your data

The most common mistake is tagging links on your own site. A UTM tag on an internal link can split one visit into two sessions, so your numbers inflate and the path a visitor took gets cut in half. Tag only the links that bring people in from outside.

The second is mixing up source and medium. Source is the place, medium is the type. Facebook is a source, social is a medium. Swap them and your reports show the same place under two different types, which is hard to read and harder to fix later.

The third is tagging everything. Your email signature, a link in a chat with a friend, the homepage link in your bio: none of these need tags. Tag the links you plan to measure and leave the rest alone. A report with twenty campaigns you never look at is no better than a report with none.

The fourth is forgetting that values are case-sensitive. It bites hardest when two people tag links: one writes Facebook, the other writes facebook, and the report splits the total in two. The lowercase switch in the builder exists for this exact reason.

Frequently asked questions

Do UTM parameters change the page?

No. The page loads exactly the same for a tagged visitor and an untagged one. The tags only change what your analytics report shows after the visit.

Do UTM tags hurt SEO?

No. Search engines ignore UTM parameters when they rank pages, and a tagged link points at the same page as the untagged one. Keep a self-referencing canonical tag on the page so search engines treat the clean address as the main one. Our guide to canonical URLs explains how.

Why is all my traffic showing as direct?

Direct is the bucket for visits your analytics tool cannot explain. Links clicked in apps, chats and emails often arrive without referrer data, so they land there. Tagging those links gives the tool the labels it needs, and the visits show up under the source and campaign you named.

Should I tag links inside my own site?

No. Tags on internal links can split one visit into two sessions, which inflates your numbers and breaks the path data. Tag only links that bring people to your site from outside.

What if I forget to tag a link?

That visit stays unattributed, and there is no way to fix a click that already happened. Tag the link the next time you share it, and check your links before you send a campaign, because a link copied from an old post carries the old campaign's name.
