What Is a Readability Score and Why It Matters for SEO

Quick answer: A readability score estimates how easy a piece of text is to understand. For most web content, aim for a Flesch Reading Ease score of 60–70 (about 8th–9th grade). Clear writing helps people stay longer and makes your pages easier for AI answer engines to use.

A readability score is a number that predicts how difficult a text will be for an average reader. The most common formulas are Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level. Both look at average sentence length and average syllables per word. You can check any page in seconds with our free Readability Score Checker.

How the scores work

Flesch Reading Ease produces a number from 0 to 100. Higher scores mean easier reading. Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level estimates the U.S. school grade needed to understand the text. A score of 8.0 means an average eighth-grader should follow it without trouble.

  • 90–100 — Very easy (5th grade)
  • 60–70 — Standard (8th–9th grade) — ideal for most websites
  • 30–50 — Difficult (college level)
  • 0–30 — Very difficult

Why readability matters for SEO

Google has never said it ranks pages by a readability formula. What it does reward is content that people find useful and easy to consume. Clear writing tends to improve:

  • Time on page
  • Bounce rate
  • Return visits
  • Shares and links

These user signals, combined with relevance and authority, influence rankings over time.

Why readability matters for GEO (AI search)

Generative engines such as Google AI Overviews, Perplexity and ChatGPT prefer text they can parse cleanly. Short sentences, clear structure, and everyday language make it easier for these systems to extract accurate answers and cite your page. Improving readability is one of the simplest GEO improvements you can make.

How to improve a low score

  • Break long sentences into two or three shorter ones.
  • Prefer common words over rare or academic ones.
  • Use active voice (“We tested the tool”) instead of passive (“The tool was tested”).
  • Keep paragraphs to three or four sentences.
  • Add headings and bullet lists so readers can scan.

A worked example: rewriting one hard paragraph

Take this sentence, the kind that often appears in a guide: “The implementation of comprehensive optimisation strategies necessitates the utilisation of multifaceted analytical methodologies in order to facilitate enhanced search engine visibility.” It is grammatically correct, but it is hard work to read. Almost every word is long, and the main point is buried at the end.

Here is the same idea in plain English: “To rank better, use a clear plan, check what is working, and fix what is not.” The meaning is identical, but the second version is shorter, uses everyday words, and puts the point first. That is the whole trick: say the important thing first, then add detail only if the reader needs it.

Try this on one of your own pages. Pick the longest paragraph, rewrite it in two or three short sentences, then paste both versions into the Readability Score Checker and compare the scores. If the score jumps by ten points or more, the rewrite was worth it.

Common readability mistakes to avoid

A low score usually comes from a handful of habits, not from the topic itself. Watch for these:

  • Writing sentences longer than 25 words. If a sentence needs two commas and an “and” to hold it together, split it.
  • Using jargon to sound expert. Words like “leverage”, “synergise” or “operationalise” rarely add meaning; “use”, “combine” and “run” usually do the job.
  • Hiding the point. Starting with background and saving the answer for the last line forces readers to work for it. Lead with the answer instead.
  • One long block of text. A wall of 200 words with no heading or break is hard to scan, even if every sentence is simple. Break it up, and check the length with the Word Counter as you go.

Readability targets by audience

Not every page should aim for 60–70. A help article for the general public should be easier to read than API documentation written for developers. Readers bring different background knowledge, so set the target to match the audience, not a single number for the whole site.

  • Blog posts and landing pages: 60–70. This is the standard range for most web content, and it works for a mixed audience.
  • Product pages and checkout help: 70–80. People are scanning, not studying, so short sentences and plain words matter even more.
  • Technical docs and developer guides: 50–60. The reader already knows the subject's terms, so a lower score is fine — forcing the text down to grade 8 would strip out the words they expect.
  • Legal and medical pages: 40–50. These topics are naturally harder, but a plain-language summary at the top still helps everyone, including search engines.

The mistake is chasing one number across the whole site. Set a target per page type, check each page against its own audience, and compare similar pages against each other. A developer guide at 55 is not worse than a blog post at 65 — it is written for different readers.

What the other formulas measure

Flesch is the most common formula, but it is not the only one. A few others show up in checkers, and each counts something slightly different:

  • Gunning Fog Index. Estimates the years of schooling needed to understand a text, based on sentence length and words with three or more syllables. A Fog score above 12 usually means the text is hard for general readers.
  • SMOG Index. Counts long words in a sample of 30 sentences. It is often used for health and safety material, where a misunderstood warning has real consequences.
  • Coleman-Liau Index. Uses letters per 100 words instead of syllables, so it works better on text full of abbreviations, product names and brand terms.
  • Automated Readability Index (ARI). Uses characters per word and words per sentence. It is cheap to compute, which is why many tools show it next to the Flesch score.

You do not need to track all four. Pick one — Flesch is the usual choice — and use the others only when a client or a style guide asks for a specific one. What matters is that the score moves in the right direction after each edit, not which formula you watch.

Sentence-length fixes that move the score fastest

If your score is low, sentence length is usually the biggest lever. Word choice matters, but one 40-word sentence drags the average down more than a few long words. Three fixes cover most cases:

  • Split at “and” and “but”. A sentence joined by “and” is often two sentences in disguise. “The tool checks your page and reports the score and suggests fixes” becomes “The tool checks your page. It reports the score and suggests fixes.”
  • Move the point to the front. “After you have finished writing the draft, which should take about an hour, you should run the checker” becomes “Run the checker after you finish the draft.” Same facts, half the words.
  • Cut throat-clearing. Openings like “It is important to note that” and “In order to” add words without meaning. Delete them and the sentence usually still works.

After each fix, paste the text into the Readability Score Checker again. A single round of splitting long sentences often lifts a page by 10–15 points — more than a word-swap pass on its own.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good readability score for SEO?

Aim for a Flesch Reading Ease score between 60 and 70. That roughly matches an 8th–9th grade reading level and works well for most blog posts, product pages and guides.

Does Google use readability scores for ranking?

Google does not publish a direct readability ranking factor. However, clear writing improves user signals (time on page, bounce rate) and makes content easier for AI systems to parse and cite.

How do I improve a low readability score?

Use shorter sentences, simpler words, more active voice, and break long paragraphs. Avoid jargon when a plain word will do.

Is Flesch-Kincaid the same as Flesch Reading Ease?

They are related. Flesch Reading Ease gives a 0–100 score (higher = easier). Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level estimates the U.S. school grade needed to understand the text.

What readability score should a technical article aim for?

A Flesch score of 50–60 is reasonable for technical content. Readers of developer docs or research summaries already know the subject's vocabulary, so forcing the text down to grade 8 would strip out the terms they expect. Aim for clarity within your audience's level, not a single number for the whole site.

Can a readability score be too high?

Yes, if it comes at the cost of meaning. A score of 90 or above on a technical topic usually means you have removed the words your readers actually need. Short sentences help, but keep the terms, numbers and details that make the page useful.

Conclusion

Readability is not a magic ranking lever, but it is a practical quality signal for both human readers and AI systems. Keep most web content around the 60–70 Flesch range, use the free checker to spot hard passages, and your pages will be easier to read, share and cite.
